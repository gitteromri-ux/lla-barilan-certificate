Insights/Course 2 · Food as Medicine – Nutrition for Lifelong Health

Nutrition and health

Food as medicine, from dietary pattern trials to equitable practice

What trials and global burden studies say about dietary patterns, why cost and culture shape what people eat, and where an advisor's role ends and clinical nutrition begins.

The phrase food as medicine is now common in health policy. The American Heart Association (AHA) describes Food Is Medicine as the provision of healthy food resources to prevent, manage or treat specific clinical conditions in coordination with the health care sector 9. For Longevity and Lifelong Wellbeing Advisors, the broader lesson is that everyday eating patterns shape long-term health. Applying that lesson well means reading the evidence carefully, taking cost and culture seriously, and knowing where advisory support ends and clinical nutrition care begins.

The burden of suboptimal diet

The Global Burden of Disease 2017 diet analysis estimated how much noncommunicable disease could be attributed to dietary risks across 195 countries 1. In 2017, 11 million deaths and 255 million disability-adjusted life-years were attributable to dietary risk factors 1. High sodium intake, low intake of whole grains and low intake of fruits led the list globally, with an estimated 3 million, 3 million and 2 million deaths respectively 1. The authors note that dietary data came from mixed sources and were not available for all countries, which increases the uncertainty of the estimates 1. These are modeled population figures, useful for setting priorities rather than for predicting any individual's risk.

In the United States, the gap between guidance and practice is wide. The AHA advisory observes that most of the population does not eat in line with recommended guidelines, and it names barriers including food and nutrition insecurity, food marketing, and the access to and affordability of healthy foods 9.

Patterns, not single nutrients

Some of the most persuasive evidence comes from trials of whole dietary patterns. In the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) trial, 459 adults ate a control diet for three weeks and were then randomly assigned for eight weeks to the control diet, a diet rich in fruits and vegetables, or a combination diet rich in fruits, vegetables and low-fat dairy products with reduced saturated and total fat 2. Sodium intake and body weight were held constant. The combination diet lowered systolic and diastolic blood pressure by 5.5 and 3.0 mm Hg more than the control diet, and by 11.4 and 5.5 mm Hg more among the 133 participants with hypertension 2.

The PREDIMED trial in Spain assigned 7,447 adults aged 55 to 80 at high cardiovascular risk, but without cardiovascular disease, to a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil, a Mediterranean diet supplemented with nuts, or a control diet with advice to reduce dietary fat 3. Its history is instructive. The investigators withdrew their 2013 report after identifying protocol deviations, including enrollment of household members without randomization, and in 2018 republished revised analyses that did not rely solely on the assumption that every participant had been randomly assigned 3. The revised hazard ratios for major cardiovascular events were 0.69 for the olive oil group and 0.72 for the nut group, and results were similar after excluding 1,588 participants whose assignments were known or suspected to have departed from the protocol 3. Strong findings can survive scrutiny, but advisors should know when a landmark study has been corrected.

Processing has also been tested directly. In an inpatient crossover trial at the NIH Clinical Center, 20 adults ate ultra-processed and unprocessed diets for two weeks each, with meals matched for presented calories, energy density, macronutrients, sugar, sodium and fiber 4. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about 508 kcal more per day and gained 0.9 kg, while they lost 0.9 kg on the unprocessed diet 4. The trial was small and short, so it points to a mechanism worth attention rather than to long-term outcomes.

What current guidance asks of people

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025–2030 summarize their message as eat real food, meaning diets built on whole, nutrient-dense foods, including protein foods, dairy, vegetables, fruits, healthy fats and whole grains, paired with a dramatic reduction in highly processed foods 5. They set sodium below 2,300 mg a day for people aged 14 and older and saturated fat at no more than 10 percent of daily calories, while acknowledging that more high-quality research is needed on which dietary fats best support long-term health 5. They also note that some older adults need fewer calories but equal or greater amounts of key nutrients such as protein, vitamin B12, vitamin D and calcium 5.

WHO describes a variety of minimally processed and unprocessed foods low in unhealthy fats, free sugars and sodium as the foundation of any healthy diet 6. Crucially for practice, it adds that the exact make-up of a healthy diet varies with individual characteristics, cultural context, locally available foods and dietary customs 6.

Equity, cost and culture

Guidance assumes that people can obtain the foods it recommends. USDA's Economic Research Service estimates that in 2024, 13.7 percent of US households, 18.3 million, were food insecure at some time during the year, and 5.4 percent had very low food security 7. Rates were far higher in households with incomes below 185 percent of the poverty threshold (32.8 percent) and in households with Black (24.4 percent) or Hispanic (20.2 percent) reference persons 7. Households with an adult aged 65 or older had a below-average rate of 9.9 percent, yet 24.1 percent of food-insecure households included an older adult 7.

Cost is part of the picture. A meta-analysis of 27 studies from 10 countries found that the healthiest food-based diet patterns cost about $1.48 more per day than the least healthy, in 2011 international dollars 8. For a household on a tight budget, that difference is not trivial.

These findings shape practice in every setting. In a workplace wellbeing program, an advisor might work with facilities staff to make affordable whole foods the easy default in cafeterias and vending machines rather than relying on individual willpower. In a community program for older adults, cooking sessions built around familiar, culturally meaningful dishes, with links to food assistance where needed, respect both cultural context 6 and the reality of food insecurity 7. In a one-to-one session, a client's grocery budget, cooking facilities and food traditions belong in the assessment from the start.

A dietary plan that a client cannot afford, prepare or recognize as their own food is unlikely to last, however strong the evidence behind it.

Where advisory practice ends

Food is not a substitute for clinical care, and the AHA advisory itself cautions that relatively few Food Is Medicine studies have designs that provide strong evidence linking these interventions to health outcomes or costs 9. Several situations call for referral. Medicare Part B covers medical nutrition therapy for people with diabetes or kidney disease, or who have had a kidney transplant in the last 36 months, when a doctor refers them, and only a registered dietitian or a nutrition professional who meets certain requirements can provide it 10. The Dietary Guidelines likewise advise people with a chronic disease to talk with a health care professional about adapting the guidance to their needs 5. Eating disorders are serious and can be life-threatening, and people with them are at higher risk of medical complications and co-occurring mental illness 11. An advisor who notices signs of disordered eating, or whose client raises diet questions tied to a medical condition or medication, should refer rather than advise.

In practice

How an advisor applies this

An advisor helps clients translate population guidance into realistic, culturally familiar routines, exploring budget, cooking facilities, food access and preferences during a non-diagnostic assessment. Plans emphasize small, sustainable shifts toward whole dietary patterns, and connecting people with community food resources is part of the work when cost or access is the barrier.

Scope note

Boundaries and referral

Advisors do not prescribe therapeutic diets or provide medical nutrition therapy. Clients with diabetes, kidney disease or other chronic conditions, signs of disordered eating, or diet questions linked to medication should be referred to a physician or registered dietitian.
