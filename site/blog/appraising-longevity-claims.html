Evidence appraisal

Appraising longevity claims, from study design to supplement labels

A practical framework for separating evidence-based longevity practices from claims that lack sufficient scientific support, built on study design, endpoints and regulation.

One-page summary

Longevity is a crowded marketplace of claims: supplements said to slow aging, tests that promise a biological age, regimens extrapolated from a single animal study. Clients, employers and community partners will ask advisors what to make of them. Distinguishing evidence-based longevity practices from claims that lack sufficient scientific support is therefore a core professional skill. It does not require a statistics degree. It requires a handful of questions, asked consistently, about how a claim was tested and what was actually measured.

Start with the study design

Different designs answer different questions. Laboratory and animal studies explore mechanisms. Observational studies, such as cohort and case-control studies, follow people as they live and can reveal associations across large populations and long periods. Randomized controlled trials assign an intervention by chance and lack most of the biases that limit observational research 5.

The GRADE approach turns these differences into a judgment about the quality of a body of evidence, meaning confidence that an estimate of effect is correct, rated as high, moderate, low or very low 2. Randomized trials begin as high and observational studies as low, but either can move: quality may be rated down for risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness or publication bias, and several factors can raise confidence 2. Murad and colleagues capture this by redrawing the classic evidence pyramid with wavy lines between designs and by treating systematic reviews as a lens through which evidence is viewed, rather than as the top layer 1.

Design What it can show Main caution
Laboratory and animal studies Mechanisms and plausible targets Effects may not carry over to people 5
Observational studies Associations in large populations over long periods Confounding, including healthy-user effects 3
Randomized controlled trials Whether an intervention causes a change May be short or rely on surrogate outcomes 7,8
Systematic reviews A synthesis across studies Heterogeneity and analytic choices can change conclusions 1

When associations meet trials

The antioxidant story is the classic warning. Laboratory and animal studies showed that antioxidants could prevent the kinds of free radical damage associated with cancer 5, and observational research linked diets rich in carotenoid-containing fruits and vegetables, and high blood levels of vitamin E and beta-carotene, with a lower risk of lung cancer 4. The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study then randomly assigned 29,133 male smokers aged 50 to 69 in southwestern Finland to vitamin E, beta-carotene, both or placebo for five to eight years 4. Lung cancer incidence was 18 percent higher among men who received beta-carotene, and total mortality was 8 percent higher 4. In a second trial, CARET, beta-carotene combined with retinol increased the risk of lung cancer and of death from all causes, and the trial was ended early 5.

Why do observational findings mislead? One reason is what Shrank and colleagues call the healthy user effect, a bias arising from the behaviors and underlying characteristics of people who choose preventive interventions 3. They note that numerous highly publicized observational studies of prevention reported exaggerated relationships that randomized trials later contradicted 3.

Large trials can also deliver clear null results. VITAL randomized 25,871 US adults, men aged 50 or older and women aged 55 or older, to vitamin D3 at 2,000 IU a day or placebo 6. Over a median of 5.3 years, invasive cancer was diagnosed in 793 people in the vitamin D group and 824 in the placebo group, a hazard ratio of 0.96 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.88 to 1.06, and major cardiovascular events were similarly unchanged 6. Reading the raw counts and the confidence interval, not only the headline, is part of appraisal.

Surrogate endpoints and biological-age tests

Many longevity claims rest on markers rather than on outcomes people experience. Fleming and Powers distinguish clinically meaningful endpoints, direct measures of how people feel, function and survive, from surrogate endpoints such as laboratory values, physical signs and imaging, and explain why biomarkers can fail to predict the true balance of benefit and harm 7. Their classic example is the Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial, in which drugs given to suppress arrhythmias after a heart attack tripled the death rate, likely through effects the arrhythmia marker did not capture 7. Surrogates can also flatter. In a study of 185 trials published in six high-impact journals, trials with surrogate primary outcomes reported larger treatment effects than trials with patient-relevant outcomes, a difference not explained by risk of bias 8.

Biological-age tests are surrogates too. Moqri and colleagues, writing for the Biomarkers of Aging Consortium, note that the lack of standards and consensus on what makes an aging biomarker reliable hinders validation for clinical use 9. Measurement noise is a practical concern: technical noise produced deviations of up to 9 years between replicate samples for six prominent epigenetic clocks, although a computational fix brought most replicates within 1.5 years 10. A consumer test reporting that someone is three years younger after a new regimen may reflect noise rather than change.

Supplements and the regulatory gap

Many longevity products are sold as dietary supplements, which are regulated very differently from drugs. Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act, FDA does not have the authority to approve supplements before they are marketed, and firms generally need not provide FDA with the evidence they rely on to substantiate safety 11. The main exception is a new dietary ingredient, which requires notification at least 75 days before the product is introduced 11. Labels may carry structure/function claims, but these must be accompanied by a disclaimer that FDA has not evaluated the statement, and the Federal Trade Commission oversees advertising 11,12.

Quality is a further question. NCCIH notes that what is on the label may not be what is in the product; FDA has found prescription drugs, including anticoagulants, in products sold as supplements 12. Some supplements interact with medicines, and St. John's wort, for example, makes many medications less effective 12. Terms such as standardized, verified or certified do not necessarily guarantee quality or consistency 12.

A claim is only as strong as the outcome it measured and the design that tested it.

A practical appraisal checklist

A consistent set of questions turns these principles into routine practice.

  1. Who was studied? Cells, animals or people, and are the people similar to the client or group in front of you?
  2. What was the design, and how certain is the overall body of evidence 2?
  3. What was measured, a surrogate marker or how people feel, function and survive 7?
  4. How large was the effect in absolute terms, and how wide is the confidence interval 6?
  5. Has it been replicated, and what do independent reviews conclude 1?
  6. Who benefits from the claim, and are commercial interests disclosed?
  7. What could go wrong, including side effects, product quality and interactions 12?

The checklist travels across settings. A workplace wellbeing program offered biological-age testing by a vendor can ask for evidence of reliability and of links to health outcomes before committing budget 9,10. A community group of older adults asking whether everyone should take vitamin D can be walked through what VITAL found and encouraged to raise individual questions with their physicians 6. In a one-to-one session, a client excited by a headline about a compound that reversed aging in mice can be shown the difference between a promising hypothesis and an established practice, and the conversation can return to behaviors with stronger evidence.

In practice

How an advisor applies this

When a client, employer or community group brings a longevity claim, the advisor helps place it on the evidence spectrum, explaining what kind of study supports it, what was measured and how large the effect was. The advisor then redirects effort toward practices with stronger support and, when a product or test could affect health or interact with medication, encourages a conversation with a physician or pharmacist.

Scope note

Boundaries and referral

Appraising evidence is not clinical advice, and advisors do not prescribe supplements or alter medication. Questions about specific products, drug interactions or test results belong with licensed professionals.
