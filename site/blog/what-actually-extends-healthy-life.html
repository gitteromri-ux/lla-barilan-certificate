Science of longevity

What actually extends healthy life, and what remains unproven

Healthspan, not lifespan alone, is the goal, and the evidence on how to extend it ranges from well established to emerging to unsupported.

People everywhere are living longer. By 2030, one in six people in the world will be aged 60 or over, and by 2050 that age group is expected to double to 2.1 billion 1. Longer lives have not automatically meant healthier ones. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that the proportion of life spent in good health has remained broadly constant, implying that the additional years are lived in poor health 1. For professionals who help people age well, the target is therefore healthspan, not lifespan alone. This article maps what the evidence supports, what is still emerging and what is being marketed ahead of the science.

Lifespan, healthspan and the gap between them

Lifespan is the number of years a person lives. Healthspan is the number of those years lived in good health, often estimated for populations as health-adjusted life expectancy. Using WHO data for 183 member states, Garmany and Terzic found that the gap between the two widened over two decades to 9.6 years globally 2. The gap was 2.4 years wider for women than for men, and the United States had the largest gap of the 183 countries, 12.4 years, which the authors linked to a rise in noncommunicable diseases 2.

Averages conceal wide variation. WHO stresses that there is no typical older person: some 80-year-olds have physical and mental capacities similar to many 30-year-olds, while others decline much earlier 1. Some of this variation is genetic, but WHO attributes most of it to physical and social environments, such as homes, neighborhoods and communities, together with personal characteristics such as sex, ethnicity and socioeconomic status 1. For advisors, this is a useful corrective. Chronological age says little about what a person can do, and circumstances matter alongside individual effort.

What the biology of aging tells us, and what it does not

At the biological level, WHO describes aging as resulting from the accumulation of molecular and cellular damage over time, leading to a gradual decline in physical and mental capacity and a growing risk of disease 1. One framework for organizing this biology is the hallmarks of aging. In a 2023 review, López-Otín and colleagues proposed twelve: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, disabled macroautophagy, deregulated nutrient-sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation and dysbiosis 3. Each fulfills three premises: it manifests with age, experimentally accentuating it accelerates aging, and intervening on it offers an opportunity to slow, stop or reverse aging 3. The authors also describe the hallmarks as interconnected 3.

The framework is a research map, not a shopping list. That cellular senescence appears on it does not mean that a product claiming to target senescence improves human health. For advisors, the decisive questions are whether an intervention has been tested in people and whether it changed outcomes that matter to them.

What population studies show about modifiable factors

The strongest human evidence for healthy longevity still concerns familiar behaviors. Li and colleagues analyzed two large US cohorts, the Nurses' Health Study and the Health Professionals Follow-up Study, documenting 42,167 deaths over up to 34 years of follow-up 4. They defined five low-risk factors: never smoking, a body mass index of 18.5 to 24.9, at least 30 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous physical activity, moderate alcohol intake and a high-quality diet 4. Compared with adults who had none of these factors, those with all five had a hazard ratio for all-cause mortality of 0.26, and their projected life expectancy at age 50 was 14.0 years longer for women and 12.2 years longer for men 4. These are observational associations, based on self-reported behavior in cohorts of mostly white health professionals, so they indicate the scale of the opportunity rather than a guaranteed individual result 4.

Physical activity shows how such evidence becomes guidance. WHO estimates that 31 percent of the world's adults, 1.8 billion people, do not meet recommended activity levels, that inactivity increases after age 60, and that insufficiently active people have a 20 to 30 percent higher risk of death 5. WHO's 2020 guidelines recommend 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity a week for adults 6. For older adults they add a strong recommendation for varied multicomponent activity emphasizing functional balance and strength training on three or more days a week, to enhance functional capacity and prevent falls 6. Some activity is better than none 6.

Modifiable risk also extends beyond individual behavior. The 2024 Lancet Commission on dementia estimated that around 45 percent of dementia cases are potentially preventable by addressing 14 modifiable risk factors across the life course, adding untreated vision loss and high LDL cholesterol to its earlier list 7. Several of these factors, including less education, hearing loss and social isolation, depend as much on schools, services and communities as on personal choices.

Established, emerging and unsupported

Longevity claims do not all rest on the same footing.

Evidence status Examples What the evidence shows
Established Not smoking, regular physical activity, strength and balance training in later life, diet quality Large cohorts and WHO guidance link these to lower mortality, better function or both 1,4,5,6
Emerging Calorie restriction; omega-3, vitamin D and exercise assessed with DNA-methylation clocks Randomized trials show small effects on risk factors or biomarkers of aging; whether slowing these biomarkers prevents disease or extends life is not established 8,9,10
Unsupported for longevity Multivitamins taken to extend life Most research shows multivitamins do not lengthen life or lower the chance of cancer, heart disease or diabetes 11

The CALERIE trial shows what careful emerging evidence looks like. Healthy adults aged 21 to 50 without obesity were randomly assigned to a prescribed 25 percent calorie restriction or their usual diet for two years 8. The restriction group actually cut intake by an average of 11.9 percent, lost an average of 7.5 kg and improved all measured conventional cardiometabolic risk factors, including LDL cholesterol and blood pressure 8. A post hoc analysis found that the intervention slowed the pace of aging measured by one DNA-methylation algorithm, DunedinPACE, but did not significantly change other estimates such as PhenoAge and GrimAge, and effects were small 9. Calorie restriction is established to increase healthy lifespan in multiple species, yet the investigators note that a conclusive test of the geroscience hypothesis, the idea that slowing the biology of aging can delay multiple chronic diseases, will require long-term trials measuring chronic disease and mortality 9. Similarly, a post hoc analysis of the DO-HEALTH trial in 777 older adults found that omega-3 supplements slightly slowed three of four clocks over three years, with overall effects of 2.9 to 3.8 months 10.

Marketed products are another matter. NIH's National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that most research shows multivitamins do not help people live longer, and that supplements bought in stores or online may differ in important ways from products tested in studies 11.

The most reliable levers for a longer healthspan are also the least novel. Advisory practice earns trust by starting there.

What this means for advisory practice

In a workplace wellbeing program, the evidence favors investing in activity for the whole workforce, including breaking up prolonged sitting, which WHO recommends reducing 6, and easy routes to smoking-cessation support, over offering biological-age tests as a perk. In a community program for older adults, classes that combine balance, strength and aerobic activity reflect a strong WHO recommendation 6, and the social contact they provide addresses another modifiable factor 7. In a one-to-one advisory session, a client asking about a new anti-aging supplement can be shown where it sits on the evidence map, encouraged to discuss it with a physician or pharmacist, and helped to refocus on goals with stronger support.

Throughout, the role is non-clinical. Advisors assess without diagnosing, co-create plans, support behavior change and review progress, and they refer clients to licensed professionals when a question moves into clinical territory.

In practice

How an advisor applies this

A Longevity and Lifelong Wellbeing Advisor uses this evidence map to set priorities with clients, putting established behaviors first, framing emerging science as research and naming unsupported products as such. In a structured, non-diagnostic assessment the advisor explores activity, diet, tobacco use, social connection and living environment, then co-creates a plan of small, measurable steps and reviews progress over time.

Scope note

Boundaries and referral

Advisors do not diagnose disease, prescribe treatment or alter medication. Clients with symptoms, chronic conditions, medication questions or an interest in supplements should be referred to a physician or other licensed professional.
