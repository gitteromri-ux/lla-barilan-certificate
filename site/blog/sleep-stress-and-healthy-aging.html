Sleep and stress

Sleep and stress regulation as foundations of healthy aging

Short sleep is common, and chronic stress leaves measurable traces in later life. Advisors can support realistic routines, but insomnia, apnea and depression need clinical care.

Sleep and stress are easy to treat as lifestyle extras. The evidence places them closer to the foundation. In 2022 the American Heart Association added sleep health as a new, eighth component of its cardiovascular health construct, Life's Essential 8 1, and stress physiology describes how years of adaptation leave a mark on the aging body. This article sets out what advisors need to know about both, and where clinical care begins.

How much sleep adults need, and how many fall short

A 2015 consensus statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommends that adults sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis to promote optimal health 2. Regularly sleeping less was associated with weight gain and obesity, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease and stroke, depression and increased risk of death, and with impaired immune function and a greater risk of accidents. More than 9 hours may be appropriate for young adults, people recovering from sleep debt and people with illnesses; for others, its health risk is uncertain 2.

Two caveats matter for practice. The recommendation was developed for adults aged 18 to 60, so it is not an age-specific target for later life. And duration is only one dimension: healthy sleep also requires good quality, appropriate timing and regularity, and the absence of sleep disturbances or disorders 2.

Many adults fall short. In 2020 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System data analyzed by CDC researchers, an age-adjusted 33.2 percent of US adults reported sleeping less than 7 hours a day, including 34.5 percent of those aged 45 to 64 and 26.0 percent of those 65 and older 3. A meta-analysis of 16 prospective studies with more than 1.38 million participants found that self-reported short sleep was associated with a 12 percent higher risk of death and long sleep with a 30 percent higher risk 4. These associations describe population risk, not the effect of changing one person's sleep.

Stress, allostasis and the aging body

Bruce McEwen's 1998 review in the New England Journal of Medicine described the long-term costs of stress 5. Allostasis is the ability to achieve stability through change, as the autonomic nervous system, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and the cardiovascular, metabolic and immune systems respond to demands. Allostatic load is the wear and tear that results from chronic overactivity or underactivity of these systems. It can arise from frequent stress, failure to adapt to repeated stress, a response that fails to shut off, or an inadequate response that other systems must compensate for 5.

A person's response depends largely on how they perceive a situation and on their general physical health, which reflects lifestyle as well as genes. The effects of chronic stress may be exacerbated by a rich diet, tobacco and alcohol and reduced by moderate exercise, and McEwen wrote that isolation and lack of control in the work environment appear to be two important causes of allostatic load 5.

In the MacArthur Studies of Successful Aging, a score built from 10 biological parameters was measured in 1,189 adults aged 70 to 79 6. Higher scores were associated with significantly increased 7-year mortality and with declines in cognitive and physical functioning, independent of sociodemographic characteristics and baseline health status 6. The lesson is not that advisors can measure stress biology. It is that working conditions, relationships and daily habits are bound up with how the body adapts over time.

Insomnia is a clinical disorder with a clinical first-line treatment

Not all poor sleep is a disorder, and the distinction defines the advisor's scope. The American College of Physicians (ACP) describes insomnia as dissatisfaction with sleep quantity or quality, associated with difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep and early-morning waking 7. For a diagnosis, symptoms must cause clinically significant distress or impairment, occur at least 3 nights a week for at least 3 months, and not be linked to other sleep, medical or mental disorders. About 6 to 10 percent of adults meet diagnostic criteria. Insomnia is more common in women and older adults, and older adults more often report trouble staying asleep than trouble falling asleep 7.

The ACP recommends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the initial treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia disorder, a strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence 7. CBT-I combines cognitive therapy, behavioral interventions such as sleep restriction and stimulus control, and education such as sleep hygiene, and trained clinicians or mental health professionals can administer it 7. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2021 guideline also strongly recommends multicomponent CBT-I and suggests that clinicians not use sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy 8.

A review of the empirical evidence found that the direct effects of individual sleep hygiene recommendations on sleep remain largely untested in the general population 9. Tip sheets have a place in education, but they are not a treatment for insomnia.

What mindfulness programs can and cannot promise

A meta-analysis of 47 randomized trials of meditation programs, with 3,515 participants and active control groups, found moderate-strength evidence that mindfulness programs improved anxiety, depression and pain 10. Evidence for improved stress or distress was of low strength. For sleep, positive mood and attention, the review found low-strength evidence of no effect or insufficient evidence, and meditation was not shown to be better than active treatments such as medication, exercise or other behavioral therapies 10.

The authors concluded that meditation programs can produce small to moderate reductions in multiple negative dimensions of psychological stress 10. That is the right expectation to set with an employer or community partner: a useful option among several, not a cure for insomnia, anxiety disorders or depression.

Recognizing when to refer

Advisors need a clear sense of which signals to pass on, and to whom.

Signal Why it matters Next step
Loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing, often first noticed by someone else Possible sleep apnea; diagnosis may require a sleep study 11 Encourage evaluation by a physician
Trouble falling or staying asleep at least 3 nights a week for 3 months, with distress or impairment Consistent with chronic insomnia disorder; CBT-I is first-line treatment 7,8 Refer to primary care or a CBT-I clinician
Persistent sad or empty mood, loss of interest or changed sleep, nearly every day for at least 2 weeks Possible depression, which needs professional assessment 12 Encourage contact with a health care or mental health professional
Talk of death or suicide A crisis requiring immediate help 12 Connect with the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline; call 911 if life is in danger

Designing realistic routines in three settings

In a one-to-one advisory session, a 58-year-old project manager reports sleeping about six hours on work nights. The advisor asks about her schedule, daytime energy, sources of strain and what she has tried. Together they choose one change she rates as realistic, such as finishing work email at a fixed time two evenings a week, and a brief sleep log to review next session. If the log shows the persistent pattern described above, or her partner mentions loud snoring, the advisor refers rather than revising the plan indefinitely.

In a workplace wellbeing program, McEwen's emphasis on control at work points beyond individual techniques 5. An advisor might pair an optional mindfulness course with a review of after-hours messaging norms and shift scheduling, evaluated through participation and employee feedback rather than promised health outcomes.

In a community program for older adults, a session on sleep can distinguish occasional poor nights from a persistent pattern and explain when to see a doctor. Because people may not know about their snoring or breathing pauses until someone tells them 11, inviting partners helps. And because older adults may report a lack of emotions rather than a depressed mood 12, a participant who describes feeling flat deserves the same attention as one who reports sadness.

Advisors do not prescribe sleep. They make sleep and stress visible, help people change what is within their control and refer promptly when a problem needs clinical care.

In practice

How an advisor applies this

A Longevity and Lifelong Wellbeing Advisor explores sleep timing, daytime functioning and sources of strain through structured, non-diagnostic questions. Advisor and client then co-create one or two realistic changes, track them with a simple log and review progress together. In workplace and community programs, the advisor also looks at conditions such as control over work, scheduling and social contact, not only individual techniques.

Scope note

Boundaries and referral

Advisors do not diagnose sleep disorders or deliver CBT-I. Suspected sleep apnea, persistent insomnia or depression calls for referral to a physician or mental health professional, and suicidal thoughts call for the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline or 911.
