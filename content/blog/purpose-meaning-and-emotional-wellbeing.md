Wellbeing has more than one dimension. Reviewing wellbeing and health at older ages, Steptoe and colleagues note that the two are closely linked and distinguish three aspects: evaluative wellbeing, or life satisfaction; hedonic wellbeing, feelings such as happiness and sadness; and eudemonic wellbeing, a sense of purpose and meaning in life [1]. This article focuses on the third. Purpose can be explored and supported without straying into clinical work, which makes it promising territory for advisors, provided the limits are clear.

## What the cohort evidence shows, and what it cannot

In the Health and Retirement Study, 6,985 US adults older than 50 rated their purpose in life with a 7-item questionnaire [2]. Over the following four years, those in the lowest purpose category had more than twice the all-cause mortality hazard of those in the highest (hazard ratio 2.43), with a similar association for deaths from heart, circulatory and blood conditions [2]. In the Midlife in the United States study, purposeful individuals lived longer over 14 years of follow-up, even after controlling for other markers of psychological and affective wellbeing [3]. The benefit did not appear to depend on age, on how long participants lived during follow-up or on whether they had retired [3].

Purpose also appears relevant to cognitive aging. Among 951 community-dwelling older people without dementia, followed for up to 7 years in the Rush Memory and Aging Project, greater purpose was associated with a substantially reduced risk of Alzheimer disease (hazard ratio 0.48) [4]. A person scoring at the 90th percentile was about 2.4 times more likely to remain free of the disease than one at the 10th percentile. The association persisted after accounting for depressive symptoms, neuroticism, social network size and chronic medical conditions [4].

These are observational findings, and the field's own reviewers are cautious. In the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, 29.3 percent of people in the lowest quartile of eudemonic wellbeing died over an average of 8.5 years, compared with 9.3 percent in the highest quartile, independent of age, gender, demographic factors and baseline mental and physical health [1]. Yet the authors point to confounding, to reverse causality, since a person reporting poor wellbeing may already be ill, and to publication bias, and they conclude that alternative explanations cannot be ruled out [1]. Purpose is best presented as a promising health asset, not a proven treatment.

## Transitions that test purpose

Purpose is often renegotiated at transitions. Earlier research documented a negative association between retirement and purpose, suggesting that retirement could leave people feeling aimless [5]. A quasi-experimental analysis of 8,113 American adults, using Social Security incentives to isolate the effect of retiring, instead found a sizable increase in purpose after retirement, driven by people with lower socioeconomic status who retired from dissatisfying jobs [5]. What a person is leaving matters as much as what comes next.

Caregiving presents a similarly mixed picture. A landmark study by Schulz and Beach reported higher mortality among strained spouse caregivers, but five later population-based studies found reduced mortality and extended longevity among caregivers as a whole, compared with non-caregivers [6]. Most caregivers also report benefits from caregiving, and many report little or no strain. The reappraisal's authors argue for targeting services to caregivers who are highly strained or otherwise at risk [6].

Bereavement calls for restraint as well as vigilance. Grief is not a disease, and most people adjust without professional psychological intervention, yet bereavement is associated with excess mortality risk, particularly in the early weeks and months after a loss [7]. Intervention, the reviewers conclude, should be targeted at high-risk people and those with complicated grief or bereavement-related depression and stress disorders [7].

## Interventions with modest effects

Positive psychology interventions, delivered as self-help, group training or individual therapy, were assessed in a meta-analysis of 39 randomized studies with 6,139 participants [8]. Effects were small, with standardized mean differences of 0.34 for subjective wellbeing, 0.20 for psychological wellbeing and 0.23 for depression, and they remained small but significant for wellbeing at three to six months. Heterogeneity was high, there were indications of publication bias and study quality varied considerably [8].

Volunteering has been advocated by the United Nations and by American and European governments as a way to engage people in their communities [9]. In a systematic review, cohort studies linked volunteering to favorable effects on depression, life satisfaction and wellbeing, but not on physical health, and experimental studies did not confirm these findings [9]. A meta-analysis of five cohort studies found a lower mortality risk among volunteers (risk ratio 0.78), but the causal mechanisms remain unclear [9]. Both approaches are reasonable options within a co-created plan, offered with honest expectations.

## Measuring wellbeing without diagnosing

The WHO-5 Well-Being Index asks about the past two weeks through five statements, such as having felt cheerful and in good spirits and having woken up fresh and rested [10]. Each is rated from 0 to 5, and the raw total of 0 to 25 is multiplied by four to give a percentage score. WHO notes that a percentage score below 50, or a raw score below 13, has been suggested as a cut-off for poor mental wellbeing and as an indication for further assessment for a possible mental health condition such as depressive disorder [10].

In advisory practice, that further assessment belongs to a qualified clinician. A low score is a prompt for a careful conversation and, where appropriate, a referral, never a label. Repeated over time, the same instrument can show whether a plan is helping.

## Boundaries around depression and suicide risk

Purpose work brings advisors close to emotional material, so boundaries should be explicit from the first session. According to the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), a diagnosis of depression requires symptoms most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks, and older adults may report a lack of emotions rather than a depressed mood [11]. When such signs appear, the advisor encourages contact with a health care provider or mental health professional [11].

NIMH's warning signs of suicide include talking about wanting to die or being a burden; feeling hopeless, trapped or without reason to live; and behavior changes such as making a plan, withdrawing from friends or giving away important items [12]. Help should be sought as soon as possible, particularly if the behavior is new or has increased recently. In the United States, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline is available by call or text at 988 and by chat at 988lifeline.org [12]. Advisors should know, before they need it, how they will connect a client to crisis support.

## Purpose work in three settings

In a one-to-one advisory session, a recently retired engineer describes losing the structure his job provided. The advisor explores what gave his working life meaning and which values he wants to carry forward, then co-creates a small experiment, such as a four-week trial of a volunteer role, with a WHO-5 check at the start and end. If the score is low and he describes persistent hopelessness, the conversation turns to referral.

In a workplace wellbeing program, a pre-retirement workshop can address purpose alongside finances, and caregiver support can be directed toward employees who report high strain [6].

In a community program for older adults, opportunities to contribute, such as mentoring or civic volunteering, can sit alongside clear routes to professional help for participants who are grieving or show signs of depression [7, 11].

> Purpose cannot be prescribed. It can be explored, tested in small steps and supported, and the advisor's task is to make that process deliberate.
