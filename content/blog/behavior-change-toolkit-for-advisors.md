Telling clients what to change and why is a natural starting point, but it is seldom enough on its own. Brief advice of this kind engages conscious, deliberative motivation, and its effects are typically short-lived because motivation and attention wane [4]. A Longevity and Lifelong Wellbeing Advisor needs a more structured toolkit, one that explains why behavior is hard to change and offers techniques with evidence behind them. This article outlines the core tools and the social context that shapes how far any of them can go.

## Start with a model of behavior

The Behavior Change Wheel was developed after a review of 19 existing frameworks found that none covered the full range of intervention functions or policies [1]. At its hub is the COM-B system, in which behavior arises from capability, opportunity and motivation [1]. Each has two forms. Capability can be physical or psychological, opportunity can be physical, afforded by the environment, or social, afforded by the cultural milieu, and motivation can be reflective or automatic [1].

Around this hub sit nine intervention functions: education, persuasion, incentivization, coercion, training, restriction, environmental restructuring, modeling and enablement [1]. An outer ring of seven policy categories, ranging from guidelines and fiscal measures to environmental and social planning and service provision, can enable those interventions [1].

The practical value lies in matching the tool to the barrier. Consider employees in a workplace wellbeing program who want to be more active but sit in back-to-back meetings with nowhere pleasant to walk. Education alone is unlikely to help, because the barrier is physical opportunity, which points toward environmental restructuring such as walking meetings or accessible stairwells. A retiree who lacks confidence with strength exercises may instead need training that builds physical and psychological capability.

## Name the active ingredients

Once the target is clear, advisors need precise ways to describe what they do. The Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy v1 was built through a Delphi-type exercise in which 14 experts rated labels and definitions of 124 techniques drawn from six classification systems, and another 18 experts grouped them by similarity [2]. The result was 93 distinct behavior change techniques clustered into 16 groups [2]. For practitioners, the taxonomy offers a shared language. A session record stating that the client agreed on a specific plan and received a self-monitoring tool supports evaluation far better than a note that exercise was discussed.

## Build habits and plan for obstacles

Habits are actions triggered automatically by contextual cues associated with their performance, such as putting on a seatbelt after getting into a car [4]. In a study of habit formation in daily life, 96 volunteers chose an eating, drinking or activity behavior to perform every day in the same context, such as after breakfast, for 12 weeks [3]. Automaticity rose quickly at first and then leveled off toward a plateau [3, 4]. Among participants whose data fit the model well, the median time to reach 95 percent of that plateau was 66 days, with a range of 18 to 254 days, and missing a single opportunity did not materially affect the process [3]. Even among these motivated volunteers, about half did not perform the behavior consistently enough to reach habit status [3]. Simpler actions become habitual more quickly, and the popular claim that habits form in 21 days is a myth [4].

Implementation intentions complement habits. They are if-then plans that specify in advance when, where and how a person will act, in the form "If situation Y arises, then I will do X" [5]. A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests involving 8,461 participants found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, with an effect size (d) of 0.65 [5].

In a community program for older adults, these tools combine naturally. A participant might anchor balance exercises to an existing routine, such as after morning coffee, record them on a simple tick sheet [4], and plan that if the class is canceled, she will walk the corridor of her building instead.

## Use conversation well

Motivational interviewing was introduced in 1983 to help people with alcohol problems change their drinking and was later developed into a collaborative conversation style for strengthening a person's own motivation and commitment to change [7]. It aims to explore and resolve ambivalence about a health behavior, encouraging people to say why and how they might change [7]. In a one-to-one session, that might mean asking a client what a daily walk would make possible for them and reflecting back the reasons they give, rather than listing the benefits of exercise.

A 2005 meta-analysis of 72 randomized trials concluded that, in research settings, motivational interviewing outperformed traditional advice-giving across a broad range of behavioral problems and diseases, with significant effects on body mass index, total cholesterol, systolic blood pressure and blood alcohol concentration, although combined estimates for cigarettes per day and HbA1c were not significant [6]. Psychologists and physicians obtained an effect in about 80 percent of studies, compared with 46 percent for other health care providers, and the authors concluded that more than one encounter helps ensure effectiveness [6].

The 2018 overview of 104 reviews, including 39 meta-analyses, offers a sober counterweight. Most meta-analytic evidence was graded low or very low quality, and moderate-quality evidence of small, mainly short-term benefits appeared in only 11 of 155 comparisons, including reduced alcohol consumption and binge drinking and increased physical activity participation [7]. The authors also call for better monitoring and reporting of intervention fidelity [7]. Motivational interviewing is therefore best treated as a respectful, skilled way of talking that requires training and practice, not as a guarantee of change.

## Behavior in its social context

Individual tools operate within social conditions. WHO's Commission on Social Determinants of Health set out three principles of action: improve the conditions of daily life, tackle the inequitable distribution of power, money and resources, and measure the problem, evaluate action and expand the knowledge base [8]. Healthy People 2030 defines social determinants of health as the conditions in the environments where people are born, live, learn, work, play, worship and age, grouped into five domains: economic stability, education access and quality, health care access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context [9].

The stakes are large. Between 2001 and 2014, the gap in US life expectancy at age 40 between the richest and poorest 1 percent of individuals was 14.6 years for men and 10.1 years for women [10]. In the lowest income quartile, geographic differences in life expectancy were significantly correlated with health behaviors such as smoking, but not significantly with access to medical care [10]. Behavior and place are intertwined.

> Behavior change is not a test of character. When a plan stalls, the first question is what the person lacked in capability, opportunity or motivation.

For advisors, this has two implications. In one-to-one work, plans must fit real constraints such as shift work, caregiving or an unsafe neighborhood. In program work, the Behavior Change Wheel points toward environmental restructuring and service provision, such as establishing support services in workplaces, alongside individual coaching [1].
